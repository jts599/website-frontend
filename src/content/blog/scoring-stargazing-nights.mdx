A clear sky is only half the story. Even on a cloudless night, a bright moon can
wash out everything worth looking at, and a late sunset can eat the best hours
before it gets properly dark. Stargazer exists to collapse all of that into one
number you can scan a calendar with.

## The inputs

Each night is described by a handful of timings and a single illumination value:

- **Sunset and the end of astronomical twilight** — when the sky actually gets dark.
- **Moonrise and moonset** — when the moon is above the horizon at all.
- **Moon illumination** — how much of the moon is lit.

None of these are hard to look up on their own. The trick is that they interact.

## Turning timings into a score

The core idea is to measure how much *dark, moonless* time a night offers
between the end of twilight and the start of the next one. A night where the
moon sets early scores well; a full moon that rises at dusk scores poorly even
though the sky is dark.

## What the score is (and isn't)

The score is a deliberately simple heuristic, not an astronomy model. It does
not know about clouds, light pollution, or local horizon obstructions. It is a
starting point for deciding *when* to look up — not a promise about what you
will see.
